# The Veteran's Desk

*Notes from forty-plus years of watching markets — and mostly watching myself. Edit this file any time; the dashboard renders it as-is.*

## Market view

I don't know what the market will do next month, and after four decades I have stopped pretending otherwise. Here's something I do know: the size of your starting stake matters far less than the length of your holding period. A modest portfolio left alone for twenty years will embarrass a large portfolio traded nervously for two.

This portfolio is built on process, not prediction — and on the honest math that you don't need expensive shares to own excellent businesses. Every holding here trades under seventy dollars, and the three core funds cost a few basis points a year. Nobody here is trying to be clever quarter to quarter. Clever is expensive, and expensive is exactly what we're avoiding.

One more thing worth knowing: most major brokerages now let you buy *fractional* shares — slices of a stock down to a dollar. If you ever outgrow this roster and want a slice of a pricier company, you don't need the full share price. The per-share number is just packaging.

## Rebalancing guidance

Prices move; targets don't. Once a year — pick a date and keep it — compare each holding's actual weight to its target. If anything has drifted more than five percentage points from target, trim it back and feed the laggards. That's it. No committee meeting required.

With a smaller portfolio, favor adding new savings to the laggards over selling the winners — it keeps trading costs (and taxes, in a taxable account) near zero. Rebalancing is maintenance, like rotating tires: necessary, unglamorous, and not something you do every morning.

One exception: if a single stock ever grows past 8% of the portfolio, or one sector past 25%, don't wait for the calendar. Trim it. Concentration is how fortunes are made and how they are lost — and this portfolio was not designed to gamble.

## Behavioral wisdom

- **Don't wait until you have "enough" to start.** Enough is a moving target that never arrives. Starting small and starting now beats starting big and starting someday.
- **The news is not your advisor.** Headlines are written to be clicked, not to be followed. If a headline makes you want to act today, that is precisely the day to do nothing.
- **Never sell in a panic; never buy in a frenzy.** Write your plan when you are calm — this page is that plan — and let the calm version of you outvote the frightened one.
- **Judge decisions by process, not outcome.** A good decision can lose money for a year. A bad decision can make money for a year. Only the process repeats. (You'll notice one holding below is underwater — that's normal, not failure.)
- **Costs are the one return you control.** Every dollar not paid in fees is a dollar compounding for you instead of someone else. That is why the core of this portfolio is measured in basis points.

## What could go wrong — honestly

No honest investor sells you a portfolio without telling you how it breaks. Here is how this one breaks:

1. **A long US bear market.** Most of this portfolio leans on American equities. In a grinding multi-year decline, diversification softens the blow — it does not prevent it.
2. **Rising rates.** Bonds are supposed to be the ballast, but when rates climb, SCHZ falls at the same time stocks wobble. The "safe" third can feel anything but.
3. **Value traps.** Cheaper stocks are sometimes cheap for a reason. Position limits keep any one mistake small, but small is not zero — Pfizer's red ink below is the reminder.
4. **Dividend cuts.** Several holdings here are owned partly for their payouts. Dividends are a policy, not a promise; in a real downturn, they get cut.
5. **Tracking regret.** This portfolio will underperform the S&P 500 for years at a time — that is the price of diversification and defense. The danger isn't the underperformance; it's abandoning the plan because of it.
6. **You.** The most reliable way to ruin a good portfolio is to override it at the worst moment. The plan only works if you let it.

> "In the short run, the market is a voting machine; in the long run, it is a weighing machine." — Benjamin Graham. Forty years on, I have found no reason to doubt him, and many reasons I wish I had listened sooner.
